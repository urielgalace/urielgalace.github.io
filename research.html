Research

When “Enough is Enough”: Corruption, Brazenness, Collective Action, and Electoral Accountability

Working paper · Dissertation chapter · Draft: May 2026 · Draft available upon request

Abstract

Why, in some cases, does learning about corruption make people more cynical and apathetic, yet in other cases lead to outrage and collective action? This paper argues that in many high-corruption environments, people do not treat corruption as a simple function of the amount stolen. Instead, what matters is the nature of the corrupt act itself: people respond when corruption becomes open and shameless. The term “brazen” captures this notion of corruption being done openly and without shame. I argue that brazen acts cross a behavioral “outrage threshold” that converts private disapproval into public action by activating moral emotions and generating common knowledge, thereby reducing uncertainty about whether others will also mobilize. I test this theory using a preregistered survey experiment with 2,125 adults in the Philippines. Respondents are randomly assigned vignettes that vary (i) the nature of corruption (quiet vs. brazen), (ii) the amount stolen, and (iii) whether stolen funds are paired with public goods or private handouts. Outcomes measure willingness to engage in collective action and voting behavior. I find that brazen acts are significantly more likely to mobilize citizens and induce electoral punishment in a high-corruption setting. I also find that brazen acts increase feelings of anger, disgust, shame, and sadness, as well as the belief others will also act. This paper contributes to the literature by identifying a mechanism that links information about corruption, emotions, and coordination beliefs to collective action and, ultimately, electoral accountability. It also offers policy-relevant insights into why transparency reforms often fail.

Working Papers

Do Political Dynasties Lead to Greater Poverty? Evidence From the Philippines

Working paper · Presented at the 2025 Philippine Political Science Association International Conference

Abstract

Do political dynasties lead to greater poverty? Though previous studies have established a theoretical link between dynastic prevalence and poverty incidence, recent empirical analyses have yielded mixed results. Theoretically, political dynasties should result in worse economic outcomes due to mechanisms such as clientelism, rent-seeking, and diminished political competition undermining public goods delivery and the quality of elected officials. Moreover, various theories have posited that poverty itself fuels dynastic dominance by fostering low-information voting and enabling vote-buying practices, thereby perpetuating a vicious cycle of political dynasties and underdevelopment. However, recent empirical studies on the Philippines cast doubt on this theoretical proposition, finding mixed or even no effect of political dynasties on poverty rates. In order to address this puzzle, this paper employs an instrumental variables regression strategy to investigate the economic impact of political dynasties. In particular, it uses the 1849 Narciso Clavería decree—a Spanish colonial-era edict which mandated that Filipinos adopt Hispanic surnames—as an instrument for political dynasties. This decree generated geographical variation in surname adoption across municipalities, which in turn is linked to the prevalence of political dynasties in different areas. Surprisingly, this study finds that municipalities with greater dynastic prevalence exhibit improved household incomes, lower poverty rates, and greater economic development today. In order to explain this counterintuitive finding, this paper theorizes that dynastic politicians have greater access to information networks, resources, and political capital that enable them to deliver improved public goods relative to non-dynastic politicians.

The Differential Impact of Colonization Within Countries: Evidence from the Philippines

Working paper · March 2025 · Presented at the 2025 Midwest Political Science Association Annual Conference

Abstract

This paper investigates the differential impact of colonization within the Philippines by exploring how early colonial institutions affect contemporary political and economic outcomes. Using the 1849 Narciso Clavería decree—which mandated the adoption of Hispanic surnames among Filipinos—as a measure for the depth of Spanish colonization, this study finds that areas that had been more deeply colonized by Spain exhibit significantly improved state capacity, higher household incomes, and lower poverty rates. In order to explain this counterintuitive finding, this paper theorizes that early colonial settlers initially organized the colony and set up institutions with the goal of extracting wealth. However, the establishment of these institutions inadvertently laid the groundwork for future productivity increases by shifting the economy from subsistence agriculture to early forms of commercialization. This created the impetus to consolidate the workforce, thereby enhancing state infrastructural power and population legibility in the process. Following the external shock of the collapse of the galleon trade, which was a critical juncture, institutions were forced to adapt, and authorities responded by liberalizing trade and opening new ports throughout the country, stimulating labor migration, population growth, and a move towards occupational specialization. This induced an urban agglomeration effect which, over time, led to the development of physical infrastructure and human capital in a virtuous circle, particularly in the post-Spanish colonial era. These effects have persisted into the present through path dependence, leading to improved outcomes in these more colonized areas today. On the other hand, deeper colonization is associated with greater levels of inequality, including greater political dynasty prevalence and a more unequal distribution of incomes, thereby counterbalancing its benefits. This research contributes to the literature by providing a novel measure of intra-country heterogeneity in the colonization experience and its corresponding impact on long-run outcomes, particularly in the underexplored context of the Philippines.

The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development: An Empirical Investigation: Revision

Working paper · December 2024

Abstract

This paper revisits the thesis of Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2001) that historical colonial institutions continue to shape contemporary economic performance. Their original instrumental variable strategy—using European settler mortality rates to identify the impact of institutions—has faced challenges, most notably from Albouy (2012), who argued against the reliability of the settler mortality data. In response, I employ a random forest regression technique to impute missing settler mortality data based on 14 geographic and climatic features. Using this imputed data, I replicate the original analysis and extend it with updated measures of institutional strength (World Bank Rule of Law index) and the most recent GDP per capita data. The findings provide mixed support for the original thesis. While the main instrumental variable results remain significant under certain specifications, the magnitude of the estimated effects often diminishes, and their significance weakens once geographic and continental controls are introduced. Overall, the results remain broadly supportive of the colonial institutions hypothesis. Although institutions have a lasting influence on economic development, their impact is more nuanced than originally suggested, with geographic factors in particular playing an important role.
