About

PhD Candidate · Sanford School of Public Policy, Duke University

I am a PhD candidate at the Sanford School of Public Policy at Duke University, where I also completed an MA in Economics en route. I study political economy, political institutions, and economic development, with a regional focus on Southeast Asia. My goal is to understand the political and economic processes that shape the differential outcomes in the levels of development among nations, as well as explore policy solutions to help narrow these differences.

My dissertation asks why it is that in some cases, learning about corruption makes people more cynical and apathetic, yet in other cases, leads to outrage and collective action. Using preregistered survey experiments, administrative data, and fieldwork across various municipalities in the Philippines, I argue that it is not so much the magnitude of corruption as the nature of the act itself-how openly and shamelessly it is conducted-that ultimately leads citizens to engage in collective action and electoral punishment.

My research has been supported by the Institute for Humane Studies and the Southeast Asia Research Group (SEAREG), among others, and has also received the best writing prize from the University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa’s Center for Philippine Studies. My work has also been discussed in news outlets such as Rappler and the Philippine Daily Inquirer.

Before Duke, I earned a Master of Global Affairs in Sustainable Development from the University of Notre Dame as a Liu Institute Fellow and an AB in Political Science from the Ateneo de Manila University. I have previously worked at the Asian Development Bank, the United Nations Development Programme, Innovations for Poverty Action, and the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, as well as served as a researcher on World Bank–assisted projects.
